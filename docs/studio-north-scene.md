# Meet Studio North before mapping the journey

Studio North is a fictional digital membership business for independent professionals. Members pay monthly for a library of reusable work templates and guided learning. Standard and premium subscriptions provide recurring revenue; optional workshops and other add-ons provide additional purchases. A member can renew or leave each month.

Picture an independent consultant preparing a client proposal. She could use a template to finish today's task faster, or work through a lesson to become better at preparing future proposals. This is an illustrative use case, not a customer observed in the data. It explains the two needs that members state before acquisition: **saving time** and **building skills**. The same service can meet different needs, so a first purchase need not imply the same reason to keep paying.

## Follow a member through the service

Discovery might begin with a search for help on an immediate task, or through a professional community or partner workshop. The prospective member then compares plans, subscribes and makes a first payment. That first positive payment establishes acquisition in our analysis; creating a CRM record does not.

The crucial next event is useful work: finding a suitable resource, using it and seeing a benefit. Saving a template is an action; completing something useful is the business promise behind it. Someone who joined to learn may need a sequence of lessons and opportunities to apply them. At renewal, members weigh the value they expect to receive against another monthly bill. Unclear plans, slow setup or too little time to use the service are possible friction points to investigate, rather than conclusions already established by the data.

Read the map from **discover → evaluate → subscribe → first value → regular use → renew**. It is a conceptual map of possible experiences. Customers need not follow an identical sequence. Billing tells us whether they paid or renewed; product activity and feedback help investigate their experience. A journey hypothesis should tell us what evidence to look for next.

## Why management needs a customer-base analysis

Management sees growth and must decide how much to spend acquiring members and how quickly to expand. Revenue alone cannot tell the team whether newer customers stay, generate sufficient contribution after service costs, or recover acquisition spending soon enough to fund further growth.

This is a question for the whole company. Operations influences the value delivered to members; finance sees the resulting stream of contribution and cash; acquisition teams decide what to spend for additional customers. Following customers from first payment through renewal connects those activities. We can understand the company's direction **from the customer up**, rather than treating customers as a marketing-only concern.

The lab therefore compares joining cohorts at the same customer age, examines differences within and across customer groups, estimates contribution value where history is incomplete, and brings that value together with current holdout-based acquisition cost. The journey helps us formulate explanations. The eight source tables let us examine which explanations the evidence supports.
