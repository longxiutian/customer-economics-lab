\Section{What would count as progress?}
Compare customers at the same age since first payment, inspect contribution after acquisition spending, then connect joining cohorts to the firm's calendar-time results. The experiment asks a separate question: did the tested campaign add value relative to its control?

\Section{Read across, then compare down}
Across a row, follow one joining cohort as it ages. Down an age column, compare different cohorts at the same stage of their relationship. A newest cohort's short history cannot be compared with an older cohort's accumulated total.
\par\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{.26\linewidth}Y@{}}\toprule
\textbf{Quantity}&\textbf{Denominator and interpretation}\\\midrule
Retained share&Retained subscriptions divided by the original acquired cohort; product inactivity is not cancellation.\\
Paying customers&People with a successful payment in the period; this is a purchase measure, not a substitute for contractual retention.\\
Cash after acquisition&Cumulative observed contribution less the cohort's tracked acquisition spending. Cash payback is the first nonnegative balance.\\\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}\par
\Boundary{First-paid month is derived from complete payment history. CRM creation, product signup and marketing attribution are separate events. Refund rows reduce value; they do not create extra orders or new people.}

\Section{Use the journey to interpret, not invent, explanations}
An onboarding event records an action. A survey rating records an expressed evaluation. Their timing can suggest a question worth investigating, but a customer path alone does not establish why someone renewed or left.
\Takeaway{Joining month defines a cohort. Stated need or age defines a segment. Customer heterogeneity exists before we choose either representation.}

\PageTitle{04 / CHANNELS AND NEEDS}{Check the trend\newline within customer groups}{Compare the same joining cohorts inside each channel and pre-acquisition need. The axes stay fixed across all four panels.}
\FullFig{channel-segment-retention.pdf}{4.7in}
\Evidence{Source: \texttt{cohort\_age\_summary.csv}. Each curve divides retained customers by its original cohort--channel--need cell. Only observed ages appear; the July 2025 curves stop at age 5. No future zeros or filled-in retention paths are added.}
\Section{What survives the breakdown?}
At observed age index 5, July 2025 retains a larger share than January 2024 in 4 of the 4 channel-by-need cells. This supports investigating improvement within the groups, rather than attributing the whole aggregate change to a different mix. The curves show where that comparison holds and where later outcomes remain unobserved.
\Section{Use the differences to ask a better question}
If a channel's aggregate curve changes, examine both its customer mix and the trajectories within the same needs. Different curves can guide a diagnostic question or a new test; they do not show that the acquisition channel caused the difference.
\Takeaway{A cohort controls the joining clock. A segment organizes customer differences. Use both before explaining a company-wide trend.}

\PageTitle{06 / FORECAST VALUE}{Make the horizon explicit\newline before comparing LTV}{Observed contribution is retained. Only the unobserved remainder of the common 12-month horizon is forecast.}
\FullFig{ltv-and-cac.pdf}{2.4in}
\Evidence{Bands are approximate 5th--95th posterior parameter percentiles. They exclude optimization, model-structure and future-economics uncertainty; they are not full predictive intervals.}
\[
\text{LTV}_{12}=\sum_{m=1}^{12}\frac{\operatorname{E}[c_m]}{(1.01)^m},
\qquad \text{net LTV}_{12}=\text{LTV}_{12}-\text{CAC}.
\]
\Evidence{$c_m$ is monthly contribution per original acquired customer, including zeros for those who have left. The first receipt is discounted at $m=1$ (saved age index 0). Future net revenue and contribution shares of payment revenue use observed channel-by-need-by-age shares, with the same group's observed renewal ages (age>=1) as fallback. Shares are fixed assumptions, not causally identified or assigned posterior uncertainty.}
\FullFig{forecast-detail.pdf}{1.7in}
\Section{Test prediction against outcomes we withheld}
Withheld cells: 258. Retained-count mean absolute error: 1.09. Revenue weighted absolute percentage error: 5.5\%. A separate training-only forecast checks complete 12-month values: LTV mean absolute error \$6.72 versus \$34.87 for its baseline. These tests evaluate prediction, not campaign effects.
For the 2025-12 cohort, forecast 12-month net LTV is \$176.83 per acquired person (5th--95th percentile: \$169.46 to \$183.95). Forecast share of LTV: 89\%.
\Boundary{The shared joining-cohort trend may miss deterioration within a particular segment; inspect the separate recent-cohort fit diagnostic. Forecast LTV and historical average CAC do not identify the causal return on the next advertising dollar. Cash payback remains undiscounted.}

\FullFig{experiment-effects.pdf}{2.45in}
\Evidence{These are point estimates over the tested window, not certain rankings. The analysis tables separately report uncertainty for the conversion-rate difference.}
\Section{Compare contribution using the same transformation}
For each arm, net revenue less variable costs gives contribution. Compare arm means per eligible prospect; then subtract the treatment's additional media cost per assigned prospect. Purchases that would have occurred without the campaign are part of the baseline.
\[
\Delta\text{contribution after media per prospect}
=\frac{C_T-M_T}{N_T}-\frac{C_C-M_C}{N_C}.
\]
\Boundary{The experimental window is not a lifetime horizon. Do not multiply a conversion lift by the average predicted LTV of observed buyers and call the result experimentally established lifetime incrementality.}
